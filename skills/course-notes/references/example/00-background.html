This section says what a vector is, and how a word becomes one.

A vector is an ordered list of numbers, such as [1.0, 0.1, 0.0, 0.0]. Its length is its dimension; that one has dimension 4. Each position can be read as a feature, a question the vector answers with a number Goodfellow et al. §2.1.

The lecture turns every token into a vector called its embedding. The simplest choice, a one-hot vector, has one position per word in the vocabulary, a 1 for this word and 0 everywhere else; it says which word it is but nothing about what the word means. Learned embeddings put words that are used alike close together slides 33–44. Real embeddings have hundreds of dimensions whose meanings nobody chose; the toy one below has four, each with a name, so it can be read.

An embedding is like a profile with scored fields: "how adjective-like, how noun-like…". It breaks because learned dimensions have no names; meaning is spread across all of them.

This section introduces matrices, their shapes, the matrix product and the transpose, then turns one token into its query with them.

0.2 vectors · 0.3 dot product

A matrix is a rectangle of numbers. Its shape is written rows × columns: the toy embedding table in 0.2 is a × matrix, one row per token, called X Goodfellow et al. §2.1.

(AB)ij = row i of A · column j of B,   (n × d)(d × m) → n × m Goodfellow et al. §2.2

The number in row i, column j of the result is the dot product of row i of the first matrix with column j of the second. The inner sizes must match; the outer sizes give the result's shape.

This section explains the exponential, then softmax on a three-number example and on the actual scores of 1.3.

The exponential ex (e ≈ 2.718) is always positive and grows fast: e0 = 1, e1 ≈ 2.7, e2 ≈ 7.4. A list of numbers that are positive and sum to 1 is a probability distribution: each number can be read as a share Goodfellow et al. §3.3.

softmax(z)i = ezi / (ez1 + … + ezn) Goodfellow et al. §6.2.2.3

Make every score positive by raising e to it, then divide each by the total so they add up to 1. Bigger scores get a much bigger share, because ex grows fast.

This section says what a layer and its weights are, then runs training on a model with a single weight so every step is visible.

0.4 matrices

A layer takes a vector in, multiplies it by a weight matrix, adds a vector called the bias, and usually applies a simple function to each number. The common one is the ReLU, max(0, x): it keeps positive numbers and turns negative ones into 0, so [−1.5, 0, 2] becomes [0, 0, 2] Goodfellow et al. §6.1. The Transformer's feed-forward network in 1.5 is two such layers Vaswani §3.3. The weights and biases together are the model's parameters.

Training chooses the parameters. It runs the model on an example with a known answer, measures how wrong the output is with a number called the loss, and changes each weight a little in the direction that lowers the loss. That direction comes from the gradient: how much the loss changes when a weight changes a little. Stepping against the gradient, over many examples, is gradient descent Goodfellow et al. §4.3.

wnew = w − η × (gradient of the loss with respect to w) Goodfellow et al. §4.3

Move the weight a small step in the direction that makes the loss go down. The size of the step is a fixed small number chosen before training.

This section explains the logarithm, then the loss Lecture 1's model is trained with.

0.5 probability distribution · 0.6 loss

The natural logarithm, written log or ln, undoes the exponential: log(ex) = x. For a probability p between 0 and 1, log p is 0 at p = 1 and falls toward −∞ as p falls toward 0 Goodfellow et al. §3.13.

loss = −log p(correct next token) cheatsheet

Look up the probability the model gave to the word that actually came next, take its log, and flip the sign so the loss is positive. A confident right answer costs almost nothing; a confident wrong answer costs a lot.

p(correct next token)
the model's probability for the right word, one number between 0 and 1
−log
minus the logarithm: 0 at p = 1, large as p nears 0

This number is the loss of 0.6 for a language model, so training pushes up the probability of each correct next word. Section 1.6 applies it to every position of the French sentence at once.
