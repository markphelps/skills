This section places the 2017 paper in the lecture's timeline, then says what problem it was solving and what it replaced.

The slide that introduces the Transformer gives three facts: it comes from the 2017 paper, it "relies on the self-attention mechanism" (self-attention: every word of a sentence looks at every other word of the same sentence, 1.3), and it reached state-of-the-art results on machine translation slide 57. The running example for the whole lecture is one translation: A cute teddy bear is reading. into Un ours en peluche mignon lit. slide 64.

What attention replaced

Before 2017 the standard tool was the recurrent network (RNN), which reads a sentence one word at a time, each step feeding the next slides 45–50. The slides list its costs: a vanishing-gradient problem and slow computation slide 53. Attention first appeared in 2014 as a patch on that design, so a translation model could look back at the input sentence it otherwise failed to "remember" slides 54–55. The Transformer's step was to keep the patch and drop the recurrence.

Tokens

Tokenization cuts text into tokens, the units the model reads; each token is then looked up as a vector (0.2). A token can be a whole word, a single character, or a piece of a word slides 25–27.

Four special tokens, markers that are not words, recur through the lecture: [UNK] for out-of-vocabulary input, [BOS] and [EOS] to mark the beginning and end of a sequence, and [PAD] to fill a batch to equal length slides 28–31. [BOS] matters later: it is the decoder's first input when translation starts slide 70.

Embeddings

Each token becomes a vector (0.2). A one-hot vector is the naive choice; word2vec instead learns an embedding by training a small network on a proxy task, predicting nearby words, over billions of words slides 33–44. Its weakness, per the slides' summary table, is that word order is ignored and an embedding is the same in every context slide 53. Attention is what makes them context-aware: the encoder's output is "context-aware encoded embeddings" slide 104.

This section starts from the lecture's picture of one query, works it through with numbers, then writes the same computation for all tokens at once, which is the formula on slide 62.

0.2 vectors · 0.3 dot product · 0.4 matrices and transpose · 0.5 softmax

The whole mechanism is three steps: score every key against the query, turn the scores into weights that sum to 1, and average the values with those weights. Everything else is how to do that for every token in parallel.

Every token gets three vectors. Its query describes what it is looking for; its key describes what it offers to be matched on; its value is the content it passes on when matched slides 58–61. Slide 60 draws the idea for one token: the query for teddy bear compared with the key of every token, including its own slides 58–60. The key is what lets the model find which tokens are most similar to the query. Slide 61 adds the third piece, a value under every token.

A library search: the query is what is typed into the search box, the keys are the labels on the books' spines, and the values are what is inside the books. It breaks in one place: attention never picks a single book; it reads a blend of all of them, weighted by match.

One query, worked through

Take a toy version of the slide's sentence, six tokens, keys and queries of size 2, and a head built to make a noun look for the word that describes it. The figure is slide 60's picture with the computed weights.

1 · Score. Multiply the query with each key (a dot product) and divide by √dk. The query for teddy bear is . Against the key of cute the scaled score is ; against its own key, ; against the rest, 0.

2 · Normalise. Softmax turns the six scores into six weights that are positive and sum to 1. cute gets ; each word with score 0 still gets , because softmax never gives exactly zero.

3 · Average. Multiply each token's value vector by its weight and add them up. The result, , is the new representation of teddy bear: mostly the content of cute, a little of everything else. That is the "weighted average of values with weights being a function of ⟨q, k⟩" on slide 99.

It is tempting to read the value as the token's actual vector, its embedding. It is not: like the query and key, it is the embedding multiplied by a learned matrix, WV slide 100 Vaswani §3.2.2. A token can be matched on one thing (its key) and contribute something else (its value).

All queries at once

Doing step 1–3 for every token is three matrix products. Stack every token's query, key and value as rows of Q, K and V:

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKᵀ / √dk) V slide 62 Vaswani §3.2.1 Eq. 1

Dot every query with every key to get a table of scores, shrink the scores by √dk, turn each row into weights with softmax, and use each row's weights to average the values. The result has one new vector per token.

Q, K, V
all the queries, keys and values, one row per token (0.4)
QKᵀ
the n × n table of every query dotted with every key
dk
how many numbers each query and key has; 2 in the toy, 64 in the paper
softmax(…) V
weights from softmax, row by row (0.5), times the values: a weighted average

Why divide by √dk

A dot product adds up dk products (0.3), so the more dimensions, the bigger the scores tend to get. The paper makes this exact with the variance, a measure of how far numbers typically spread from their average: if the parts of q and k are random with average 0 and variance 1, their dot product has variance dk, so with 64 dimensions scores get large. Large scores push softmax to put almost all weight on one token (0.5), where its gradients (0.6) are tiny and learning stalls; dividing by √dk brings the variance back to 1 Vaswani §3.2.1, footnote 4.

This section fixes the definition of a head, shows two toy heads looking for different things in the same sentence, and gives the paper's sizes.

1.3 attention · 0.4 matrices

A head is one complete attention computation, with its own WQ, WK, WV. Multi-head attention is h of them on the same input, in parallel; their outputs are glued together and mixed by one more matrix.

A head is often described as "the projection matrices that produce Q, K and V". The matrices belong to a head; they are not the head. In the paper, headi = Attention(QWiQ, KWiK, VWiV): the head is the whole attention computation done with those matrices Vaswani §3.2.2. The slide calls multi-head attention "multiple self-attention layers in parallel" slide 76.

Why several? The slide: multiple heads let the model "capture different attention features in parallel", like the several filters of a convolutional layer slide 76. The paper: they let the model "jointly attend to information from different representation subspaces" Vaswani §3.2.2.

How the heads are combined

MultiHead(Q, K, V) = Concat(head1, …, headh) WO Vaswani §3.2.2

Run h separate attention computations side by side, lay their outputs end to end into one long vector per token, and multiply by one more weight matrix to mix them.

Two heads on one sentence

Head 1 puts of its weight on cute; head 2 puts on reading. A single head given both jobs splits its weight, on each, and its output is a blur of the two. Running both heads and keeping both answers side by side is the point of multiple heads.

Lecture 2 shows that modern LLMs often share keys and values between heads: one shared K and V for all query heads (MQA) or one per group of heads (GQA), to reduce the computational overhead L2 slides 80–83 cheatsheet.

The Transformer has two halves. The encoder reads the input sentence and produces one vector per input token; the decoder writes the output sentence one token at a time, reading the encoder's vectors as it goes slides 63–72. The rest of a layer

Each encoder layer is self-attention then a feed-forward network; each decoder layer adds the cross-attention between them slides 69, 71. Around every sub-layer sit two tricks, and the model stacks N = 6 such layers on each side Vaswani §3.1.

Order has to be added

Attention has no sense of order on its own. It compares every pair of tokens the same way wherever they sit, and the model "contains no recurrence and no convolution", so without help it cannot tell "bear reads" from "reads bear" Vaswani §3.5. The fix, positional encoding, is to add a position vector to each token's embedding, either learned or hard-coded slide 68. The paper's hard-coded version uses sines and cosines of different frequencies Vaswani §3.5:

PE(pos, 2i) = sin(pos / 100002i/dmodel),   PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos(pos / 100002i/dmodel) Vaswani §3.5

Each position gets its own vector of waves: the first numbers swing quickly as the position grows, later ones slowly, so no two positions get the same pattern. That vector is added to the token's embedding.

pos
the token's position in the sentence: 0, 1, 2, …
2i, 2i+1
an even and an odd position inside the vector; even ones use sine, odd ones cosine
dmodel
the length of every token vector, 512 in the paper
sin, cos
the wave functions from trigonometry: they repeat smoothly between −1 and 1

This section explains masking, compares the two modes side by side, and counts the work each one does on the lecture's example sentence.

0.6 training and inference · 0.7 cross-entropy · 0.5 softmax and −∞ · 1.5 decoder

Same model, same weights, two ways of running it. Training: one parallel pass over a known sentence, with a mask so no position peeks at its own answer. Inference: a loop, one new token per pass, because the next input does not exist until the model writes it.

Masking means each decoder position only looks at the tokens before it; the mask that does this is called a causal mask. Slide 75 says why: future tokens have to be hidden "at training time", and building the mask as a matrix means "all operations are vectorized" slide 75. That is the key to the difference. At training time the whole French sentence is already known, so the decoder gets all of it at once, shifted right by one position behind [BOS], and the mask stops each position from seeing the word it must predict Vaswani §3.1.

What training optimises

At each position the model outputs a probability for every word in the vocabulary, and the loss is cross-entropy (0.7): minus the log of the probability given to the correct next token cheatsheet. If the model gives the correct word 0.4, that position costs ; at 0.9 it costs . 1.5 encoder and decoder · 1.6 inference loop

Take the decoder, delete the encoder and the cross-attention, and keep the masked self-attention. Train it to predict the next token of huge amounts of text. That is a GPT-style LLM.

Lecture 2 defines a large language model (LLM) by three things: billions of parameters or more, hundreds of billions of training tokens or more, and a lot of GPUs L2 slide 31. Generation is the inference loop from the previous section with a prompt instead of [BOS] alone: predict a token, append it, predict again L2 slides 90–94. Because the input is causal too, training on raw text needs no labels: every position's target is simply the next token of the text.
